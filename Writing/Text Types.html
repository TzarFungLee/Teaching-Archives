💡 ⚖️ Argumentative Essay

Purpose

To present a clear position on a debatable issue and support it with logical reasoning and credible evidence.

Structure & Format

1. Introduction (1 paragraph)
  • Hook: Start with an engaging question, fact, or statement
  • Background: Brief context about the issue
  • Thesis Statement: Your clear position on the topic ("This essay argues that...")
2. Body Paragraphs (2-3 paragraphs)
  • Topic Sentence: State one main supporting argument
  • Evidence: Use facts, statistics, examples, or quotes
  • Explanation: Show how evidence supports your argument
  • Link: Connect back to your thesis
3. Counter-Argument & Refutation (1 paragraph)
  • Acknowledge: "Some argue that..." or "It could be said that..."
  • Refute: Explain why this opposing view is weak or incorrect
  • Strengthen Position: Show how your argument is stronger
4. Conclusion (1 paragraph)
  • Restate Thesis: Remind readers of your main position
  • Summary: Briefly recap your key arguments
  • Call to Action: End with impact ("Therefore, we must...")

Key Features

  • Tone: Formal, logical, and confident
  • Tense: Present tense for statements; past tense for examples
  • Language: "In my view," "Research shows," "This evidence demonstrates," "It is clear that..."
  • AVOID: Emotional language, "I think," generalizations without evidence

Example Opening (Thesis)

"Schools should require uniforms because they reduce bullying, improve focus, and prepare students for professional environments."

💡 ✉️ Letter to the Editor

Purpose

To respond to a recent news article or issue and persuade readers and editors to consider your viewpoint.

Structure & Format

Formatting (Physical Layout)
  • Your address (top left)
  • Date
  • Publication address
  • Salutation: "Dear Editor," or "Dear Sir/Madam,"
1. Opening (1 paragraph)
  • Reference: "In response to the article 'Your Article Title' published on [date]..."
  • Your Position: State your clear stance immediately
  • Tone: Polite but firm and direct
2. Body (1-2 paragraphs)
  • Reasoning: Explain why you agree or disagree
  • Evidence: Support with relevant facts or personal experience
  • Impact: Show how this issue affects the community
3. Closing (1 paragraph)
  • Action: Call for readers/officials to "Consider," "Act on," or "Reconsider"
  • Sign-off: "Yours faithfully," or "Sincerely,"
  • Full Name, Address, Contact (Required by publications)

Key Features

  • Tone: Formal, polite, but persuasive and critical
  • Length: 150-250 words (concise!)
  • Language: "I am writing to express concern," "I strongly believe," "It is imperative that..."
  • AVOID: Emotional outbursts, insults, offensive language

Example Structure

Opening: "In response to 'Schools Cut Sports Programs' (March 15), I must express my disagreement with this policy."

Body: "Physical activity is essential for student health and mental wellness. Studies show..."

Closing: "I urge the school board to reconsider this decision."

💡 📋 Proposal

Purpose

To suggest a solution to a problem and convince decision-makers that your idea is practical and beneficial.

Structure & Format

1. Title & Introduction
  • Title: Clear and specific (e.g., "Proposal: Implementing a School Recycling Program")
  • Opening: Identify the problem concisely
  • Purpose Statement: "This proposal suggests..."
2. Background/Rationale
  • Why it matters: Explain the importance and impact of solving this problem
  • Current situation: Describe what's happening now
  • Need: Why change is necessary
3. Proposed Solutions (Main Section)
  • Use bullet points for clarity
  • Be specific: "We will establish 5 recycling stations in key locations"
  • Include timeline: "Implementation begins in April"
  • Assign responsibility: "The Environmental Club will manage..."
4. Expected Benefits
  • Advantages: List positive outcomes (environmental, financial, educational)
  • Feasibility: Explain why this solution is realistic
  • Measurable results: "We expect to reduce waste by 40%"
5. Cost & Resources
  • Budget breakdown: Specific costs if required
  • Resources needed: Materials, personnel, funding
  • Funding sources: How to pay for it
6. Conclusion
  • Summary: Reiterate the problem and solution
  • Call to Action: "We respectfully request approval to proceed..."

Key Features

  • Tone: Professional, optimistic, persuasive
  • Format: Headings, bullet points, organized layout
  • Audience: Decision-makers (principal, committee, etc.)
  • AVOID: Vague ideas, unrealistic timelines, ignoring costs

💡 📊 Report

Purpose

To present factual information about a topic or investigation in a clear, objective, and organized manner.

Structure & Format

1. Title Page / Introduction
  • Title: Descriptive title
  • Author, Date, Subject
  • Purpose: Why this report was written
2. Introduction (Overview)
  • Background: Context and relevance
  • Objectives: What the report aims to discover or explain
  • Methods: How information was gathered (survey, observation, research)
3. Findings / Results
  • Organized by topic or theme
  • Use data, statistics, tables, charts
  • Present facts objectively - no opinion here
  • Example: "78% of students prefer online homework submissions."
4. Analysis / Discussion
  • Interpret the findings
  • What do the results mean?
  • Identify patterns and connections between data
5. Recommendations
  • Suggest actions based on findings
  • Prioritize recommendations
  • Example: "The school should consider implementing digital submission for assignments."
6. Conclusion
  • Summarize key findings
  • Restate importance
  • No new information

Key Features

  • Tone: Formal, objective, impartial
  • Tense: Past tense for methods and findings; present for analysis
  • Language: "The data shows," "The survey revealed," "It was found that..."
  • AVOID: Personal opinions in findings, emotional language, bias

Sample Format

Title: "Student Satisfaction Survey Report - January 2026"

Findings: "Out of 150 students surveyed, 65% reported satisfaction with school meals."

Recommendation: "Expand vegetarian menu options to improve overall satisfaction."

💡 📰 Magazine / School Article

Purpose

To inform and engage readers about a topic of interest in an accessible and entertaining way.

Structure & Format

1. Headline (Title)
  • Catchy and attention-grabbing
  • Clearly indicates the topic
  • Examples: "Teens Master Online Learning," "New Café Opens on School Grounds"
2. Introduction (Hook Paragraph)
  • Opening sentence: Start with a question, quote, or surprising fact
  • Engage immediately: Make readers want to continue
  • Introduce topic: Briefly explain what the article is about
  • Example: "Did you know the school library is about to go digital?"
3. Body (Main Content - 2-3 paragraphs)
  • Clear paragraphs: Each covers one main idea
  • Information + Interest: Balance facts with engaging details and quotes
  • Subheadings (optional): Break up text
  • Visual elements: Mention images, statistics, quotes from people
4. Conclusion
  • Reflect on importance: Why this topic matters
  • Call to action/invitation: "Join us for..." or "Think about..."
  • Leave readers thinking: End with impact

Key Features

  • Tone: Semi-formal, engaging, accessible
  • Audience: General readers (not experts)
  • Language: Clear vocabulary, varied sentence length
  • Style: Can include quotes and personal touches
  • AVOID: Overly academic language, too formal or too casual

Example Opening

Headline: "Environmental Club's Tree-Planting Initiative Bears Fruit"

Hook: "Fifty native trees now stand on school grounds—all planted by student volunteers in just three months. Here's how they did it."

💡 🎤 Speech

Purpose

To deliver a message to an audience that is engaging, memorable, and designed to be spoken aloud.

Structure & Format

1. Opening / Greeting
  • Address your audience: "Good morning, teachers and fellow students..."
  • Thank them: Show respect for their time
  • Introduce your topic: State what you'll discuss
  • Examples: "Today I want to talk about...," "I'm here to inspire you to..."
2. Main Body (2-3 sections)
  • Use signposting: "First," "Secondly," "Finally" to guide listeners
  • Tell stories or examples: Speeches are more engaging with personal anecdotes
  • Use rhetorical devices: Repetition, rhetorical questions ("Have you ever...?"), direct address ("You can...")
  • Keep it conversational: As if speaking to friends, not reading
3. Rhetorical Techniques (Essential for Speeches!)
  • Rhetorical Questions: "Did you know...? How many of you...?" (Make audience think)
  • Repetition: Repeat key phrases for emphasis
  • Direct Address: Use "you" and "we" to connect with audience
  • Inclusive Language: "Together we can..." instead of "You should..."
4. Conclusion & Call to Action
  • Powerful closing: Restate your main message
  • Inspire action: "I urge you to..." or "Let's all commit to..."
  • End memorably: Strong final sentence
  • Thank audience: Show gratitude

Key Features

  • Tone: Confident, conversational, inspiring
  • Language: Short sentences (easier to say aloud), active verbs
  • Format: Write for speaking - use contractions ("don't," "it's")
  • Devices: Rhetorical questions, repetition, direct address
  • AVOID: Long, complicated sentences; reading directly from paper; filler words ("um," "like")

Example Opening

"Good morning everyone. I'm [Name], and today I want to talk to you about something I'm passionate about: mental health. How many of you have felt stressed or overwhelmed? If you're raising your hand right now—you're not alone. And that's exactly why we need to talk about this."

💡 📖 Short Story

Purpose

To entertain and engage readers through narrative, character development, and vivid descriptions.

Structure & Format

1. Introduction - Setting & Characters
  • Establish setting: When? Where? Use descriptive language
  • Introduce main character: Who? What do they want?
  • Create mood/atmosphere: Is it happy, tense, mysterious?
  • Example: "The abandoned house loomed in the fog, its broken windows like hollow eyes watching the street below."
2. Rising Action - Build Tension
  • Introduce the problem/conflict: What challenge does the character face?
  • Show don't tell: Describe actions and dialogue, not emotions
  • Add obstacles: Make the problem more complicated
  • DON'T write: "She was afraid." DO write: "Her hands trembled as she opened the door."
3. Climax - Peak of Action
  • The turning point: The moment of highest tension or decision
  • Character makes crucial choice
  • Action unfolds quickly
4. Falling Action & Resolution
  • Consequences unfold: What happens after the climax?
  • Resolve the conflict: Problem is solved or accepted
  • End clearly: Wrap up loose ends
5. Conclusion - Ending
  • Satisfying or thought-provoking
  • Can be happy, sad, ironic, or surprising
  • Often includes a twist or lesson
  • Options: Resolution, twist ending, cliffhanger, or reflection

Key Features

  • Tone: Varies by genre (thriller, romance, fantasy, mystery)
  • Tense: Usually past tense ("She walked," "He said")
  • Perspective: First person (I) or third person (she/he)
  • Description: Use the five senses - sight, sound, smell, taste, touch
  • AVOID: Tell don't show; one-dimensional characters; explaining every emotion

Example (Show Don't Tell)

❌ WEAK: "Marco was worried about the test and didn't study."

✅ STRONG: "Marco stared at the blank test paper, his mind a fog. The equations might as well have been written in a foreign language. He should have opened his textbook last night instead of scrolling through videos."

💡 💻 Blog Post

Purpose

To share personal thoughts, experiences, and opinions in a conversational and approachable way.

Structure & Format

1. Catchy Title
  • Engaging and personal
  • Can use questions or humor
  • Examples: "Why I Stopped Procrastinating (And Why You Should Too)," "5 Things No One Tells You About High School"
2. Introduction
  • Personal touch: Start with a personal story, question, or observation
  • Why you're writing: Share your motivation
  • Conversational tone: Talk directly to the reader (use "you")
  • Example: "So I had this moment last week where I realized..."
3. Main Content (Multiple Sections)
  • Short paragraphs: Easier to read on screen
  • Personal experiences: Share stories and examples
  • Use subheadings: Organize your thoughts
  • Include humor or emotion: Connect with readers
  • Mix fact and opinion: Research + personal take
4. Voice & Language
  • Use "I" and "you": Personal pronouns are expected
  • Contractions: "don't," "it's" (informal style)
  • Conversational phrases: "Anyway," "To be honest," "Here's the thing..."
  • Emoji or formatting (optional): Break up text visually
5. Conclusion & Call to Action
  • Wrap up your thoughts
  • Invite engagement: "What do you think?" or "Drop a comment below!"
  • Question for readers: Encourage discussion
  • Example: "I'd love to hear about your experience with this. Comment below—I'll definitely respond!"

Key Features

  • Tone: Casual, personal, friendly
  • Audience: Readers interested in your perspective
  • Structure: Flexible - can be list-based, narrative, or reflective
  • Engagement: Comment prompts, questions, personal connection
  • AVOID: Overly formal language; hiding your opinion; ignoring reader interaction

Example Structure

Title: "The Day I Realized Failure Isn't the End"

Opening: "So I didn't get into my first-choice college. Yeah, that sentence took me three days to write without getting emotional."

Body: Share personal experience, reflection, lessons learned

Closing: "If you're reading this and feeling disappointed about something—you're not alone. And it's not the end. What's your story? Tell me in the comments!"
