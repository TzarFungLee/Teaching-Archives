Passage — Text 1

Between the Bell and the Ring Light

How short-form video is reshaping hobbies for Hong Kong teens

[Adapted magazine feature — Youth Link Weekly]

[1] When the last school bell rings at Buddhist Wong Wan Tin College, Form 5 student Mei Ling Tang does not rush straight home for a nap. She checks her backpack for a small phone tripod, a clean apron, and leftover ingredients from home economics. Within an hour she is filming a sixty-second dumpling tutorial for Cliply, a short-video app popular with local teens. “I cook what my grandmother taught me,” she says, “but I edit it so friends on the MTR can watch between stations.”

[2] Mei Ling is one of thousands of Hong Kong secondary students who treat short-form video as both hobby and side project. A recent Youth Link survey of 800 S4–S6 students found that 67% watch short clips every day, while 22% have posted at least one video of their own. Most creators film after dinner or on Saturday mornings. Few expect to become full-time influencers; they want likes, language practice, and a creative break from drills.

[3] Balancing homework and filming is the hardest part. Mei Ling’s mother once found her filming at midnight and set a firm rule: finish core subjects first , then record for a maximum of forty minutes. Mei Ling keeps a simple timetable stuck on her desk. “If I skip revision for views, my English quiz scores drop,” she admits. Her classmate Jason Ho tried daily posting for a month, then paused when mock exams approached. He now uploads only on Sundays.

[4] Teachers notice both risks and rewards. Mr Chan, Mei Ling’s English teacher, encourages students to subtitle their clips in English. “Short scripts force them to choose precise words,” he explains. “That is useful practice.” He worries, however, about students comparing themselves to polished accounts with professional lighting. “A ring light cannot replace sleep,” he warns.

[5] Parents are divided. Mrs Wong, whose son posts basketball tips filmed near a Kowloon mall, allows filming only outdoors in daylight and bans face filters that hide age. “I want him safe and honest,” she says. Other parents confiscate phones during the week. Mei Ling’s grandmother is her most loyal viewer and sometimes appears stirring soup.

[6] Safety advice travels slowly. Cliply’s teen guide recommends turning off location tags, avoiding school uniform in public clips, and never meeting strangers from comments alone. Mei Ling follows these steps and also saves drafts overnight so she can check captions when she is calmer. “One angry reply used to ruin my evening,” she recalls. “Now I wait before I answer.”

[7] Short video has also changed weekend hobbies. Instead of only shopping in malls, friends film mini challenges: timed library dances, MTR exit scavenger hunts within station rules, and bilingual joke sketches. Jason Ho jokes that his football team warms up twice—once on the pitch and once for the camera. Not every experiment works. A cooking clip that failed spectacularly still earned Mei Ling her highest comment count, mostly from classmates offering better recipes.

[8] Looking ahead, Mei Ling hopes to keep creating through S6, but only if grades stay stable. She has already turned down a paid product mention from an unknown online shop. “If I do not use it myself, I will not promote it,” she says. For her, the ring light is a tool, not a career plan—yet. Between the school bell and bedtime, sixty seconds of honest cooking may be enough.
