Text 2 — Lights, cues, and cheers

[1] Welcome to CityBeat’s weekly chat with people who shape Hong Kong pop nights. Today our guest is Maya Cheung, a stage show producer for idol concerts at the Hong Kong Coliseum. Maya, how did you fall into live concerts?

[2] I grew up on Cantopop. My mum played old Leslie Cheung records while she cooked, and later my classmates pulled me into J-pop dance covers. I was never a polished dancer myself, but I was blown away by how one good light cue could make a crowd scream together.

[3] What hooked you on producing rather than performing? Funny enough, a muted phone clip. I was on the MTR with the volume off and watched a local boy group hit every step in perfect sync. The glamorous moves looked effortless. Later I played it and realised the music was only half the magic — timing and safety on stage mattered just as much.

[4] How is a Coliseum show different from filming a music video? On stage every second is live. If a dancer slips, you cannot edit it out. We rehearse safety moves for two weeks. The dancers find the timing demanding, but they enjoy the cheers. Budgets in Hong Kong are often tighter than big overseas tours, so we reuse stage pieces and keep lighting simple but bright.

[5] Fans wave light sticks and film everything. Does that change your plans? Yes. Many teens watch short clips before they buy tickets, so we design a few clear “wow” moments for small screens. Still, a phone cannot replace standing with friends in the hall. That warmth is not just about loud music. Live crowds feel wholesome: decent people sharing one song, not a moody night alone on the bus.

[6] Some people say idol concerts are only good marketing. Fair? Uh… hmm. That’s a fair challenge. I suppose… marketing fills seats, but it cannot hold a three-hour show alone.

[7] So are the performers genuinely talented? Absolutely. Hard work beats a pretty poster. My mentor once said, “Talent without rehearsal is just hope.” My brother used to joke that idols are “all made in a factory,” but he changed his mind after he saw how long the dancers train for one chorus.

[8] Can you recommend acts students might enjoy? Try LUNA WAVE — they write many of their own hooks and feel like the kings of local ballads. StarKit is the hottest girl group right now: their set is upbeat and charming. Echo Yard is a small rock band that plays its own instruments with meaningful lyrics about school stress. And NovaLee talks about loneliness in a way teens understand.

[9] What would you change about the concert industry if you could? Big companies control most venues, so it is hard for independent acts to compete. Some music videos cost a fortune. Live shows can feel addictive and exhausting for both fans and crews. Newcomers rarely find it easy to enter.

[10] Next week: Kenji Mori, a J-pop vinyl collector from Causeway Bay.

Text 3 — Never meet the encore

Posted by MayaFan_88 at 9:14 p.m.

[1] After my CityBeat interview went live, the magazine sent me two VIP passes for NovaLee’s Coliseum night. I took my friend Rita, who had never seen a full idol show. We bought light sticks on the MTR and thought the whole evening would be a dream from start to finish.

[2] Echo Yard opened with a quiet song about exam stress; Rita was truly touched and wiped her eyes. Then StarKit bounced on in funky silver jackets and made the whole floor jump. NovaLee closed the set with a slow ballad. We sat so near the stage that we could see sweat on the dancers’ faces — eww, but also cool. Their new album, Harbour Glow, will be a chart-topper for sure.

[3] Backstage I finally met NovaLee. My heart pounded and my mouth went dry. I wanted to say thank you for the song that helped me through mock exams, but only a tiny “hi” came out. NovaLee looked past me, said a flat “Hi” with no smile, and told her assistant, “The meet-and-greet is over.” The assistant gave us a soft, sorry look. Rita tried to joke, but I felt smaller than my light stick. Ouch. Never again!
