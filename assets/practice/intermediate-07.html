Text 2 — Shared pencils, shared playlists

[1] InkPop’s weekly chat meets people who keep Hong Kong’s comic and anime corners friendly. Today we welcome Priya Lam, who runs Pixel Nest, an anime café near Mong Kok that hosts weekend comic-con pop-ups. Priya, what pulls secondary students through your door?

[2] Friendship first. Friends come to draw, play board games, and talk about favourite characters. The café is not only about buying posters. I grew up on Cantopop ballads, then J-pop anime themes, and now K-pop dance covers on Friday nights. I’m a romantic about fandom rooms.

[3] What first hooked you on anime cafés? A muted cosplay clip online. Volume off, but the costumes looked so polished and glamorous that I was blown away. When I finally heard the fan chant, I realised community sound mattered as much as pretty art.

[4] How is Pixel Nest different from a normal coffee shop? Our walls show art from student clubs, and we hold quiet drawing hours after school. Guests find the tables welcoming, with shared pencils and clean paper. English comic editions can build vocabulary in a catchy, memorable way — panels stick in your mind even when the words are new.

[5] Some parents say comics waste time. What do you tell them? Many fan spaces feel wholesome: decent people sharing stories, almost no scandal talk, sweet and positive energy. I was drawn to that attitude — that’s the whole point of a kind fandom. Other online corners can feel moody or harsh.

[6] Is kindness just good marketing for a café? Uh… hmm. That’s a fair question. I suppose… cute merch helps, but it cannot build trust alone.

[7] Are young cosplayers and artists genuinely skilled? Yes. Hard work shows in every stitch. Artist Jin Ho once said, “Cute designs without practice are just luck.” My aunt used to say cosplayers were “all made in a factory,” copying the same look, but she changed her mind after our charity stall for a children’s library in Kowloon.

[8] Who should students follow this season? Try the comic circle Moon Panel — kings of gentle school stories. Glitter Frame is the hottest girl cosplay crew: upbeat and charming. Bass Alley is a small rock-band cosplay team with meaningful lyrics about friendship. And streamer PixelJay talks about loneliness in a way shy fans understand.

[9] What would you change about fan culture if you could? Big brands control most pop-up rents, so it is hard for independent artists to compete. Some promo videos cost a lot of money. Fandom can feel addictive and exhausting. Newcomers rarely find it easy to enter. Long night streams tire students — sleep matters more than extra views.

Text 3 — Autograph with no smile

Posted by PriyaNest_11 at 4:42 p.m.

[1] After my InkPop interview, the magazine sent two weekend passes for Pixel Nest’s comic-con pop-up. I took my friend Sana, who draws every lunch break. We pinned buttons on our bags and thought we would meet kindness in person at last.

[2] Student club Moon Panel opened with a quiet reading; Sana was truly touched and clapped until her palms hurt. Then Glitter Frame danced in funky orange jackets and made the café cheer. Streamer PixelJay closed the afternoon. We stood so near the table that we could see sweat on his forehead under the lights — eww, but human. His new channel series will be a chart-topper with teen viewers, I’m sure.

[3] When my turn came, my heart pounded and my mouth was dry. I wanted to thank him for a video that helped my art homework, but only “hi” came out. PixelJay checked his phone, said a flat “Hi” with no smile, and told his assistant, “The meet-and-greet is over.” The assistant gave us a soft, sorry look as the next group was waved away. Sana squeezed my arm; I wanted to sink under the table. Ouch. Never again!
